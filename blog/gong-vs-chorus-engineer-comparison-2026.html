Gong vs Chorus for speech engineers: how to compare

Updated September 2026 • 6 min read

Gong and Chorus are the two best-known names in revenue intelligence — software that records and analyzes sales conversations to surface deal risk and coaching signals. Both are built on the same core speech stack (ASR, diarization, redaction, then sentiment / topic / summarization models over the transcript), so the day-to-day engineering work is genuinely similar. The meaningful differences are structural.

A note on the numbers

We don't have inside compensation data for either company, and we won't invent it. For real, current figures, check levels.fyi, Glassdoor, and Blind, and — most reliably — ask for the range in your first recruiter call. See our salary guide for the broader market context.

The structural difference

  • Gong is an independent, venture-backed company. It raised large rounds and was valued in the billions at its 2021 peak; it has not gone public. Equity is private stock — potentially valuable, but illiquid until an IPO or acquisition, and marked to a private valuation that can move in either direction.
  • Chorus was acquired by ZoomInfo (NASDAQ: ZI) in 2021 (a deal reported at roughly $575M) and now operates as part of ZoomInfo's go-to-market platform. Equity offers are ZoomInfo RSUs — public stock you can sell as it vests, with the usual trade-off that a business unit inside a larger public company tends to move slower than a standalone startup.

If you value liquidity and predictability, the public-RSU structure is an advantage. If you're betting on upside and want a standalone product org, the independent company is the pick. Neither is universally "better."

Product and engineering focus

  • Gong is a large standalone product organization centered entirely on the conversation-intelligence product and its analytics layer. Speech and ML roles sit close to the core product.
  • Chorus is one capability within ZoomInfo's wider platform, so the work often involves integrating conversation data with ZoomInfo's contact and company data. Good if you like systems-integration problems; less so if you want a pure speech focus.

Both work on real-world audio problems: telephony-quality speech, crosstalk and overlap, diarizing two-party and multi-party calls, redacting PII, and increasingly putting an LLM over the transcript for summaries and scoring.

How to actually evaluate them

  1. Read their engineering output. Both publish engineering blogs and give conference talks — that's the most honest signal of what the work and the culture are like.
  2. Ask the recruiter for the band for the specific level and location on the first call. If they won't share it, that itself is information.
  3. In the loop, ask engineers what the on-call rotation looks like, how much of the role is speech modeling vs. platform work, and how promotion decisions are made.
  4. Compare offers on total comp and liquidity, not headline numbers — private equity and public RSUs are not the same instrument.
If you're choosing between offers

The right call depends on your risk tolerance and what you want to work on, not on a ranking. Get both bands in writing, weigh liquid vs. illiquid equity honestly, and pick the team whose engineering you'd be proud to have on your résumé.
